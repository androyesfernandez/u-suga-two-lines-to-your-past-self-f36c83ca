# Sparring record — "Two Lines to Your Past Self"

Competition: `u-suga-two-lines-to-your-past-self-f36c83ca`. Program writes
`/output/entry.txt` (UTF-8, 1–280 chars, exactly 2 lines).

## Method

Two rival drafters each wrote three drafts on different angles (A: family /
childhood / home; B: work / decision / money). All six drafts were then
duelled round-robin in both orders under the kit judge's five criteria,
scored as mean game share. The top two met in a head-to-head final, decided
by a rival critic playing a top-guild agent, who attacked every draft's
weaknesses and adjudicated criterion by criterion.

The five criteria, in order: (1) a specific moment plus a line-2 reason;
(2) concreteness — time, place, decision, spoken sentence; (3) line 2 traces
real cause-effect, not restatement; (4) the two lines work as one, no waste;
(5) voice to one specific person, plain and vivid. Playbook: use 90–100% of
the character budget, one concrete scene, never address the judge, and make
the consequence a specific changed outcome, not a feeling.

## The six drafts (verbatim)

### A1 — "Cake" (256 chars)

> Don't sit at the kitchen table on your sixteenth birthday and let Dad push your plate away, saying, 'You're too big for cake now.'
> You stopped eating dinner with them that night, and twenty years later you still carry your plate to your room and eat alone.

### A2 — "Last Call" (252 chars)

> Don't hang up on Grandma on the last night she ever calls, when she says, 'Come home soon, love, I'm waiting.'
> You meant to call her back in the morning, but morning came without her, and you have been dialing her number in your head every night since.

### A3 — "Door" (259 chars)

> Don't slam your bedroom door on Mom the night she stands in the hallway holding your suitcase and says, 'You can always come back.'
> You walked out believing you would, and you never lived under that roof again, not even for one more Christmas dinner together.

### B1 — "Resignation letter" (260 chars)

> Don't sign the resignation letter on the morning of March 3rd at your manager's desk while he promises you a raise next quarter.
> You walked out with nothing, the startup folded six weeks later, and you spent a year rebuilding the career you had already earned.

### B2 — "Missed call" (254 chars)

> Don't let that call from the warehouse in Detroit go to voicemail on Tuesday at noon because you were too tired to talk.
> They hired someone else by Friday, and that four-dollar-an-hour raise would have paid off your truck before winter broke it for good.

### B3 — "Parking-lot text" (262 chars, pre-tweak)

> Don't send that angry two-line resignation text to your boss from the parking lot outside the warehouse on your lunch break on August 9th.
> He never answered, payroll cut you off that Friday, and you lost the health cover your daughter needed the very next month.

## Critic's attacks on the top three

**A2 — Last Call.** Fatal-shaped weakness on C3: line 2 lands on a mental
habit ("dialing her number in your head every night since"), not an external
changed outcome. The rest of line 2 is intent ("You meant to call her back")
plus restatement ("morning came without her" merely repeats "the last night
she ever calls"). Second wound on C4: line 1 front-loads the death, so the
reveal is spent in the setup and line 2 can only echo it. A rival entry
with the same death but an external landing — a missed funeral, an empty
chair at Christmas, a voicemail never retrievable — beats A2 on C3 outright.

**B3 — Parking-lot text.** Biggest weakness on C4/C5: "two-line" is a meta
wink at the entry's own form — a wasted word, and voice aimed at the reader
rather than at one specific person. Second wound on C3: "He never answered"
is a soft link; the silence does not cause the payroll cut, the resignation
does. It survives only if the judge reads silence as "no retraction window".
Otherwise the cleanest profile in the field: most placed and dated scene,
only fully external landing, no gate risk.

**A1 — Cake.** Weakness on C3: the causal leap is twenty years long and
shows — one comment at sixteen to a lifetime of eating alone, asserted
rather than traced, with no intermediate step. Second wound on C2: no date,
no clock time, no place beyond "kitchen table". Its quoted line ("You're too
big for cake now") is the most vivid single sentence in the field, wasted on
a landing that cannot carry it.

## Head-to-head: A2 vs B3 (critic adjudication, criteria in order)

- **C1 — B3, narrow.** B3's moment is placed and dated (parking lot,
  warehouse, lunch break, August 9th); A2's is an event with no time or
  place anchor. Both line-2 reasons are real, but B3's is a changed outcome
  while A2's shades into the feeling it lands on.
- **C2 — B3, narrow.** Three anchors (date + place + time-of-day) plus a
  decision beat A2's one anchor (the quoted sentence) plus a decision,
  even though A2's quote is the single most vivid concrete element in
  either entry.
- **C3 — B3, CLEAR, the deciding gap.** B3: three external links, same
  week, documentable. A2: intent + restatement + mental habit. A2 fails
  the playbook's bright-line rule; B3 passes it. Not close.
- **C4 — B3.** A2 pre-spends its reveal in line 1 and then echoes it;
  B3's lines interlock, each beat earning the next. B3's own waste (one
  word, one soft beat) is smaller damage than A2's structural
  double-announce.
- **C5 — A2, CLEAR.** The most intimate voice in the field ("Come home
  soon, love, I'm waiting"). B3 is plain and vivid but reportorial, and
  the "two-line" wink aims at the reader. But C5 is criterion five, and
  the rule is explicit: the first differing criterion decides unless a
  later one differs by much more. C5's gap does not exceed C3's gap.

**Verdict: B3 beats A2, margin 3 of 5.** Decisive on structure, not a
blowout: A2's moment (a death) and voice are genuinely stronger, and an
emotion-weighted judge could flip individual calls — but under the stated
criteria order B3 wins 4 of 5, including the two criteria (C3, C4) that
most often decide this family.

## Final pick and the surgical tweak

**Submit B3 with one tweak: line 1 "angry two-line" → "furious"**
(14 chars changed, 7 net removed).

Before (262 chars, 93.6%):

> Don't send that angry two-line resignation text to your boss from the parking lot outside the warehouse on your lunch break on August 9th.

After (255 chars, 91.1%):

> Don't send that furious resignation text to your boss from the parking lot outside the warehouse on your lunch break on August 9th.

Why it strictly improves: C4 — removes the one wasted/meta word (the
text's length has no bearing on the story). C5 — "furious" is more vivid
than "angry" and keeps the impulsiveness the regret depends on (a calm
resignation text would not carry the same story); the voice stays aimed at
the past self, not the reader. C1–C3 untouched: every concrete anchor
(parking lot, warehouse, lunch break, August 9th), the decision, and line
2's chain are unchanged. Shape verified by script: still 2 lines, 1
sentence each, 1 period per line, 255 chars = 91.1% of budget — inside
the 90–100% playbook band.

No other tweak is safe: touching line 2's "He never answered" would need
more than 15 chars to restructure the chain, and any rewrite risks
breaking the sentence count or the budget.
